Three of the twelve are worth arguing about. One of them is the one you have already started, and the honest recommendation is a graft rather than a restart.

Keep A-01, graft the safety spine of A-03 onto it

Access control where a confirmed fire alarm overrides every lock — the same system, one new authority question, seven existing ADRs preserved.

Why the graft

The decision currently blocking ADR-0004 is fail-safe versus fail-secure per door class. That is not an access-control question at all — it is the fire-egress question. A-03 is where that question comes from, so grafting it does not add scope, it supplies the missing rationale for a decision you already owe.

You gain the fire and smoke effects, the egress routing and the moving occupants — the visual demo A-01 lacks — and a genuine Simplex split: a safety controller that unlocks egress on alarm, sitting above a policy engine that would otherwise keep doors shut.

In exchange, the fault-injection and unsafe-control-action sections of the report write themselves, because the STPA table has an obvious top entry: the access service holds a door locked while the zone behind it is filling with smoke.

A-01

Zone access control with a coupled air-quality loop

Badges open doors; the people who walk through them poison the air; the air budget decides who gets in next.

your current picktwo loopsscope risk
Control loop
SenseBadge swipes, door position sensors, per-zone CO₂ and occupant count
DecidePolicy engine: is this credential permitted, and does the zone have air budget left?
ActLock state on the door; ventilation rate on the zone AHU
PhysicsAdmitted people raise CO₂ by mass balance; fan flow removes it
CO₂ rise constrains the next admission

Physical model

Well-mixed CO₂ mass balance per zone:

V·dC/dt = n·G + Q·(C_out − C)

with G ≈ 0.005 m³/h of CO₂ per seated adult, Q the supply airflow set by the actuator, V from the room's floor area × 2.7 m. Occupancy is a weekday schedule — arrivals 07:00–09:00, a lunch trough, departures 16:00–18:00 — plus booked meetings and a random no-show rate.

Decision route

Rules with explicit state, not a classifier. Permission check against a versioned policy, then an admission guard: refuse or throttle entry when projected CO₂ at +15 min exceeds 1500 ppm. Hysteresis on the guard so the door does not chatter.

AI layer, advisory only: an anomaly detector over swipe sequences (tailgating, impossible travel, out-of-hours patterns) that raises alerts and never issues a lock command — a provenance assertion in the tests proves it.

Aggregates

  • Zone — capacity, occupancy, air budget; invariant: occupancy never exceeds capacity
  • Door — position, lock, door class; invariant: an egress door is never left locked
  • AccessPolicy — versioned rule set, immutable once published
  • AdmissionDecision — the event, carrying inputs, policy version and outcome

Containers

  • badge-reader ×N — device processes, publish swipes
  • door-gateway — sole writer of BuildSim door state
  • access-service — policy and admission decisions
  • air-simulator — CO₂ physics and occupancy
  • vent-actuator — applies fan commands
  • anomaly-detector — advisory alerts
  • broker, store, dashboard

Evaluation & breaking point

p95 swipe→unlock latency; denial correctness; recall/false-positive rate of the detector on a labelled replay day.

Breaking point: raise the shift-change swipe rate until p95 crosses 500 ms — then say which container saturated, and prove it with a flame graph or a queue-depth plot.

Risks

Two loops means two simulators; the air loop is easy to under-build and then it is decoration. Ration your time to it explicitly.

BuildSim's PUT /api/doors replaces the whole collection — there is no per-door endpoint. That forces a single-writer gateway, which is an architecture decision you must state rather than discover in week 5.

A-03

Fire growth, sprinkler suppression and egress routing

A fire grows, smoke fills rooms, sprinklers fight it, locked doors disappear from the walkable graph and people are re-routed around the smoke.

best demosafety architecturelargest scope
Control loop
SenseHeat and smoke detectors per room, door position and lock state, occupant positions
DecideSafety controller (alarm → unlock egress) above a router that avoids smoke-filled rooms
ActSprinkler valves, door locks, per-occupant route assignment
Physicst² fire growth, smoke filling and spread along room adjacency, water suppression
Water lowers heat release; smoke changes the next route

Physical model

Heat release rate follows the standard growth law, suppressed exponentially once water is applied:

Q̇(t) = α·t², then Q̇ ← Q̇·e^(−k·t_water)

Smoke fills a room as a rising layer and spreads to adjacent rooms through the existing floor graph; obscuration in a room sets a detector reading and a visibility value that the router treats as impassable above a threshold.

Decision route

This is the course's runtime-assurance pattern made literal. A safety controller — dumb, fast, always right — unlocks every egress door and sounds the alarm on a confirmed detection. Above it, an advanced controller computes per-occupant routes via GET /api/graph/route, avoiding smoke.

The safety controller can veto the router; the router can never keep a door locked. That is a Simplex architecture you can draw in one minute at the whiteboard, which is exactly what the oral rewards.

AI layer

Early fire classification by sensor fusion — heat rate-of-rise plus smoke obscuration plus CO — against a single-threshold baseline. The metric that matters is false alarms per simulated week versus detection delay, because a fire alarm that unlocks the whole building has a real cost when it is wrong. That trade-off curve is a first-class report figure.

Containers

  • fire-simulator — growth, smoke, suppression
  • detector ×N — device processes per room
  • safety-controller — alarm and egress authority
  • evacuation-router — route assignment
  • door-gateway, sprinkler-actuator
  • occupant-simulator — moves entities along assigned routes
  • broker, store, dashboard

Evaluation & breaking point

Time from ignition to full egress; occupants who ever enter a smoke-filled room (should be zero); detection delay; false-alarm rate; water used.

Breaking point: increase simultaneous evacuees until route recomputation cannot keep up with the 1 Hz tick. BuildSim caps at 2 000 entities per snapshot, which gives you a hard ceiling to plot against.

Risks

The obvious one is scope — bound it to one floor, one ignition point, three egress doors, 80 occupants and say in the proposal that you did.

Smoke spread is easy to over-model. A two-zone layer model per room is defensible; a CFD ambition is not, and the guide explicitly rewards honesty about a simple model over silence about a complex one.

A-04

Daylight-harvesting lighting on a Luleå winter

Dim the lights to whatever the sun is not providing — in a town where the sun provides three hours in December and twenty-one in June.

low effortclean energy baselineneeds depth added
Control loop
SenseCeiling lux sensor, PIR presence, wall-switch overrides
DecideHold 500 lux at the desk while occupied; predict dwell to avoid nuisance switch-off
ActPer-luminaire dim level 0–100 %
PhysicsDaylight through the window by solar position, plus artificial contribution
Dimming changes measured lux directly

Physical model

E_room = E_daylight(t, orientation, cloud) + d·E_max

Solar altitude for latitude 65.6° N gives you a genuinely dramatic seasonal signal for free, and a cloud process as a slow random walk gives the controller something to fight. Occupancy comes from the same weekday schedule every idea on this sheet shares.

What stops it being trivial

Three things, and you must build all three or the project is thin: PIR sensors that miss stationary people, so raw presence is unreliable and needs a dwell model; the lux sensor reading its own lamp, which is a positive feedback path that will oscillate if you use a naive proportional controller; and a manual override that must decay back to automatic.

That oscillation is the best thing in this idea — it is a real stability argument you can plot, explain, and fix with a deadband.

Evaluation & breaking point

kWh against always-on and against a plain occupancy-only baseline; minutes below 300 lux while occupied; nuisance switch-offs per day; oscillation amplitude before and after the deadband.

Breaking point: luminaires per controller until the loop period slips.

A-05

Cleaning-robot fleet dispatch under battery and door constraints

Rooms get dirty at a rate set by footfall; robots have finite charge and cannot pass a locked door. Decide who cleans what, in what order.

excellent demoscheduling, not controltwo coupled states
Control loop
SensePer-room soil level, robot battery state of charge, robot position, door lock states
DecideAssign rooms to robots; decide when to break off and dock
ActRoute command and cleaning mode per robot; dock/undock
PhysicsSoil accumulates with footfall, drops when cleaned; charge drains with work, recovers at the dock

Physical model

soil ← soil + β·person_minutes − clean_rate·Δt SoC ← SoC − (p_move + p_clean)·Δt, at dock: + p_charge·Δt

Two coupled physical states with genuinely different time constants — soil in hours, charge in tens of minutes — which is what makes the scheduling interesting rather than arbitrary.

Decision route

Baseline: greedy nearest-dirtiest with a fixed charge reserve. Contender: an assignment formulation over the next horizon that respects battery feasibility and travel distance from GET /api/graph/route. Both are defensible; the report's job is to show whether the second one earned its complexity, and the guide is explicit that it might not have.

The door coupling is the sharp edge: a robot dispatched to a room behind a locked door must detect infeasibility rather than stall, which is a beautifully concrete stale-state problem.

Evaluation & breaking point

Mean and worst-case soil level; rooms exceeding their threshold overnight; metres travelled per room cleaned; battery-depletion events (must be zero).

Breaking point: robots × rooms until assignment latency exceeds the dispatch period — a clean, honest scaling curve.

A-06

Ventilation fault detection, diagnosis and graceful degradation

The damper is stuck and the sensor is drifting. Both look like bad air. Tell them apart, then keep the building comfortable anyway.

deep dive ↓grade-5 evidence built instrong AI fitmodest demo
Control loop
SenseCO₂ per zone, commanded vs. measured damper position, fan power, supply temperature
DecideDemand-controlled ventilation, plus a residual monitor that classifies the fault
ActDamper and fan commands; on a diagnosed fault, compensate elsewhere and raise a work order
PhysicsCO₂ mass balance with a fault model injected between command and effect
Compensation changes the residual the monitor sees

The core idea

Run the same mass balance the controller uses as an observer. Command a known ventilation step and predict the CO₂ decay. The residual between predicted and measured decay separates the faults:

  • Stuck damper — no decay follows the command; fan power unchanged
  • Drifting sensor — decay has the right shape but the wrong offset
  • Blocked filter — decay is slower than predicted, and fan power is higher for the same flow

Three faults, three distinguishable signatures, one residual. This is a real diagnostic argument and it fits on a whiteboard.

Decision route

CUSUM on the residual as the baseline. The contender is a small autoencoder or one-class model over a window of (command, flow, power, CO₂) — evaluated against the baseline on detection delay and false alarms per week, on identical seeds. If it loses, say so; the guide values that more than a win.

Why it grades well

The report guide asks for fault injection and quantitative evidence for the higher grades. Here fault injection is the use case, so the experiment section is not bolted on. You get detection delay, ROC, comfort-during-fault and recovery time as first-class results rather than as an afterthought.

A-07

Peak-demand load shedding with a comfort constraint

The building has a power ceiling and a price signal. Pre-heat before the peak, shed during it, and never let a zone leave its comfort band.

cleanest numbersoptimisation defence
Control loop
SenseZone temperatures, aggregate power draw, hourly price, outdoor temperature
DecideWhich zones heat in the next interval, subject to a kW cap and comfort bands
ActPer-zone heater enable and setpoint
PhysicsFirst-order RC thermal model per zone with solar, occupancy and envelope loss
Shedding cools the zone, which raises its priority next interval

Physical model

C·dT/dt = (T_out − T)/R + P_heat + q_occupancy + q_solar

One RC pair per zone, identified from a step response you run yourself — which is itself a nice experiment section. Price data from a public SE1 series makes the objective concrete and local.

Decision route

Baseline: priority shedding — coldest zone first, hard kW cap. Contender: a small linear program over a 2–4 hour horizon that pre-heats into the peak using thermal mass as storage. The pre-heating behaviour is visible, explicable and genuinely clever without being opaque.

The defence you must prepare: why is the LP feasible in 200 ms, and what do you do in the interval where it is not? Have a fallback to the priority rule and measure how often it fires.

Evaluation & breaking point

kWh, peak kW, cost in SEK, comfort-minutes violated — each against the baseline on identical weather and occupancy seeds. Trade-off curve of cost against comfort.

Breaking point: zones × horizon length until solver time exceeds the control period. This is the most legible scaling story of the twelve, because the failure is a hard timeout, not a slow degradation.

A booking is a forecast of occupancy. Ventilate ahead of it, and if a room physically cannot meet the air target in time, book a different room.

The LLM, done defensibly

Natural-language booking requests — “somewhere for eight people Thursday morning, near the lab” — parsed by LangChain4J into a validated, structured request object. Everything the course notes demand is naturally present: an allowlisted tool set, schema-validated arguments, a step and time limit, a deterministic form-based fallback, and injection handling on untrusted text.

A-09

Water-leak detection, localisation and isolation

Flow in does not equal flow out. Find which branch is leaking, close the right valve, and dispatch someone — while water spreads through the floor plan.

nobody else will pick itstrong visuals
Control loop
SenseBranch flow meters, pressure, floor-level water sensors per room
DecideLocalise the leak from the flow imbalance; choose the smallest valve set that isolates it
ActClose zone valves; dispatch a maintenance entity; warn affected rooms
PhysicsLeak flow, water spreading to adjacent rooms, drainage
Closing the valve drops inflow, so levels stop rising

The inference problem

A tree of branch meters plus a mass balance at each junction localises a leak to a branch without any machine learning — and the interesting part is that meters are noisy and a small leak hides inside the noise band. The trade-off between time to detect and false isolations is the whole engineering story, and it has an obvious plot.

Isolation is a graph problem: find the minimum valve cut that separates the leak while keeping the most fixtures in service. Two lines of reasoning, one satisfying answer.

Physical model

V_room ← V_room + (q_leak − q_drain)·Δt, overflow spills to adjacent rooms

Spread uses the same adjacency graph everything else uses, so you build one topology and reuse it. Depth per room drives both the sensors and the room layer in the viewer.

Evaluation & breaking point

Time from leak start to isolation; litres spilled; rooms wetted; fixtures unnecessarily taken out of service; false isolations per simulated month.

Breaking point: shrink the leak rate until detection time exceeds your requirement — a sensitivity floor, which is a more interesting breaking point than a throughput one.

Why the loop is real

Most predictive-maintenance projects are open-loop classifiers with a dashboard. This one closes because the action — derating — feeds back into the degradation process and into the comfort loop it was meant to serve. That conflict is the project.

You are inventing the degradation physics; there is no bearing in BuildSim. A-11

Outdoor-air damper: particulates against CO₂

One actuator, two objectives that pull in opposite directions. Open the damper and CO₂ falls but wildfire smoke comes in; close it and the air gets clean but stale.

best value for efforta Pareto plot of your own
Control loop
SenseIndoor PM2.5 and CO₂, outdoor PM2.5 from a replayed dataset, occupancy
DecideOutdoor-air fraction and filter/recirculation mode, under a hard CO₂ ceiling
ActDamper position 0–100 %, fan speed
PhysicsTwo mass balances sharing one flow term — the coupling that creates the conflict
Every damper move helps one pollutant and hurts the other

Why this is the sharpest small idea

Two coupled equations, one shared control input:

V·dC/dt = n·G + Q_oa·(C_out − C) V·dPM/dt = Q_oa·(1−η)·PM_out − (Q_oa + Q_filter·η)·PM

The same Q_oa appears with opposite sign in the two objectives. That single fact gives you a constrained trade-off, a Pareto front, a hard safety constraint (CO₂ ≤ 1500 ppm always wins) and an argument about which objective is negotiable — all from about eighty lines of simulator.

Decision route

Baseline: fixed outdoor-air fraction, the way most real buildings run. Contender: a weighted-objective controller with the CO₂ ceiling as a hard constraint, plus filter-mode switching. Sweep the weight to produce the Pareto front — that figure alone answers the report's demand for measured trade-offs.

Outdoor data can be replayed from a public air-quality series with a wildfire episode in it, which satisfies the hybrid simulation route the proposal guide suggests.

Evaluation & breaking point

Cumulative PM exposure in µg·h per occupant; CO₂ exceedance minutes; fan energy. All three against the fixed-fraction baseline on identical episodes.

Breaking point: raise outdoor PM until no damper position satisfies both constraints — and then explain which one you sacrifice and why. That moment is the most interesting sentence you can put in a final report.

A-12

Self-tuning optimal-start heating

At −20 °C outside, how early must heating start so the room is at 21 °C exactly when the first person arrives? Learn the answer from yesterday's mistake.

smallest defensible projectlearning that earns its placequiet demo
Control loop
SenseZone temperature overnight, outdoor temperature, first-arrival time
DecidePreheat lead time from the current temperature gap and outdoor conditions
ActStart heating at the computed hour; hold setback until then
PhysicsRC warm-up from setback with envelope loss and heater capacity limits
Yesterday's arrival-time error retrains today's lead time

Why the learning is honest here

Classic optimal start is a two-parameter regression: lead time against temperature gap and outdoor temperature. It learns online from its own error, which is a genuine adaptive loop, tiny to implement, and impossible to hand-wave — you can derive it on the whiteboard in the oral.

It also has the honest property that a fixed two-hour start is a strong baseline. If your learner only beats it by four minutes and 3 % of energy, that is a finding worth reporting, and the course guide says so explicitly.

Where the difficulty lives

Not in the model but in the data: arrival times are noisy, some days nobody comes, and a cold snap moves the whole relationship. Drift handling, outlier rejection and a sane cold-start prior are the real engineering, and they map straight onto the course's data-quality requirement.

Evaluation & breaking point

Arrival-time error in minutes across 30 simulated days; overshoot in degree-minutes; kWh against the fixed-start baseline; convergence rate after a step change in outdoor conditions.

Breaking point: drop the outdoor temperature until the heater saturates and no lead time is sufficient — the point where the controller must instead report that it cannot meet the target, which is a behaviour worth building.

B-13

Corridor and stairwell congestion control

At noon the same three staircases take everybody. Route people around the jam without creating the next one.

uses all three floorsoscillation is the subject
Control loop
SenseOccupant counts per corridor segment and stair, queue lengths at stair heads
DecideRoute assignment and release staggering; when to open an overflow stairwell
ActPer-occupant route, guidance messages, door unlock on the overflow route
PhysicsCrowd density sets walking speed; stairs have a hard capacity in persons per metre per second
Rerouting changes density, which changes speed, which changes the next route

Physical model

The pedestrian fundamental diagram is enough, and it is citable:

v = v_free·(1 − ρ/ρ_max), flow = ρ·v

with v_free ≈ 1.34 m/s on the flat and roughly half that on stairs. Segment lengths come straight out of BuildSim's walkable graph — 2 643 nodes and 2 706 edges across three floors, with 32 cross-floor links, so the topology is given to you.

Decision route & the trap

PhysicsThe same CO₂ mass balance, with people as the hidden state

The idea in one equation

Every other ventilation project uses the mass balance forwards. Run it backwards and it becomes a sensor:

n̂ = [ V·dC/dt − Q·(C_out − C) ] / G

You have deleted a device and replaced it with arithmetic — which is precisely what embedded intelligence at the edge means, and it costs a weekend rather than six.

The subtlety worth the whole grade

Your own actuator sits inside your estimator. Ventilate hard and CO₂ flattens out, so the signal carrying the occupancy information disappears — you have made the state less observable by controlling it well. Ventilate gently and the estimate sharpens while the air gets worse.

That is a real dual-control trade-off, and there is an elegant answer available: inject a small deliberate excitation into the ventilation rate to keep the estimate alive, and measure what it costs in comfort. Very few student projects contain a sentence that good.

A-06 in depth: where exactly is the loop?

A fair question, because A-06 hides its feedback better than the others. There are two loops stacked on top of each other, and the interesting engineering is entirely in the second one — including one failure mode where the outer loop erases the evidence the inner loop needed.

The one-paragraph answer

If you only remember one thing before the oral, remember this shape.

Inner loop — control

CO₂ rises because people are in the room. The controller opens the damper. Airflow dilutes the CO₂. The next reading is lower. Actuator changes physics changes sensor — the course's required loop, unchanged from any ventilation project.

Outer loop — supervision

A model predicts what CO₂ should have done given the command that was sent. The gap between predicted and measured is the residual. A persistent residual means the command did not have its expected physical effect, so the supervisor changes how the controller controls — different strategy, different zone, work order raised.

Why that is still feedback

Because the supervisor's action changes the commands, the commands change the physics, and the physics changes the residual the supervisor reads next. It closes through the building exactly like the inner loop does — just one level up, and on a slower clock.

Figure 1 — the two loops, and where the fault is injected

Read the inner ring first: controller, damper, room, sensor, back to controller. Then the outer ring: the same command also goes to the observer, which predicts the outcome and compares. The fault sits between the command and its physical effect, which is precisely why the controller cannot see it — the controller only ever sees CO₂, and high CO₂ looks the same whether the damper is stuck or the room is simply full.

Figure 2 — what a residual actually is

The single idea the whole project rests on. You command a known ventilation step and you already know, from the mass balance, what CO₂ must do next. If it does something else, the fault is between your command and the air.

The model, forwards

V·dC/dt = n·G + Q·(C_out − C)

Volume V, occupants n, CO₂ generation G per person, commanded airflow Q. Integrate one step ahead and you have the prediction:

Ĉ(t+Δ) = C_out + (C(t) − C_out)·e^(−QΔ/V) + n·G/Q·(1 − e^(−QΔ/V))

The residual

r(t) = C_measured(t) − Ĉ(t)

Noise makes single residuals meaningless, so accumulate them. A one-sided CUSUM detects a persistent small bias far faster than a threshold on r alone:

S(t) = max(0, S(t−1) + r(t) − k), alarm when S > h

k is the slack you allow normal noise; h trades detection delay against false alarms. Those two numbers are your tuning story.

Why n is the honest problem

The prediction needs occupancy, and if you had a perfect occupancy sensor you would not need this. Three ways out, all defensible: take n from the simulator's schedule and admit it; estimate n by inverting the same balance (that is B-20, and the two ideas compose beautifully); or use only step responses, where the decay shape depends on Q and V but barely on n.

The third is the strongest answer and the cheapest to build.

The masking trap — and why it is the best answer you can give

The outer loop can destroy the evidence the outer loop depends on. Build it, watch it happen, then fix it.

What goes wrong

The supervisor compensates for the stuck damper by boosting the neighbouring zone. CO₂ in A109 comes back down. The residual shrinks. A naive monitor now sees healthy behaviour and clears the fault — so the supervisor stops compensating, CO₂ rises again, and the system oscillates between broken and fine while the damper is still stuck.

Why it happens

Because the residual is computed against the outcome rather than against the commanded path. Once compensation is active, the room is no longer being controlled by the thing you are testing, so the test is meaningless.

The fix, in three parts

Latch the diagnosis until a human clears it. Recompute the residual against the actual local command, not the zone outcome. And schedule a periodic active probe — briefly return the zone to local control, command a step, and confirm the fault is still there. That last one is deliberate excitation, the same idea that makes B-20 work.

Justify two merges you did not make: the monitor could live inside the controller, and the supervisor could live inside the monitor. Keeping them apart means a crashing monitor cannot take ventilation down, and a wrong diagnosis cannot command anything by itself. That is the fault-isolation argument the deployment section asks for, and it is true rather than decorative.

Requirements this generates

  • R-1 When a damper fails to respond, the system shall confirm the fault within 15 min. Verify: injection test × 20 runs, report p95 delay.
  • R-2 While a fault is latched, the system shall keep the zone below 1000 ppm by compensation. Verify: scenario test.
  • R-3 The monitor shall raise no more than one false alarm per simulated week. Verify: 7-day fault-free replay.
  • R-4 While readings are stale, the monitor shall report unavailable and issue no diagnosis. Verify: kill the sensor container.
  • R-5 Every diagnosis shall be reconstructable from stored evidence. Verify: rebuild a random one from the journal.

Experiments, in build order

  • Detection delay against CUSUM limit h — the core trade-off curve
  • ROC over the three fault types, model-based versus a plain threshold baseline
  • Comfort held during a fault, with compensation versus without
  • Sensitivity: smallest damper deviation still detectable, as a function of sensor noise
  • Breaking point: zones per monitor until the residual computation misses its interval

Honest weaknesses to write down

You built the fault model and the detector, so the ROC measures your detector against your own generator — say it plainly, as A-10 must too.

The observer needs occupancy, and every way of getting it is imperfect. The step-response method is the least bad and you should say why.

Three fault types is not a taxonomy. Real air handling fails in dozens of ways, several of which look identical to your three.
